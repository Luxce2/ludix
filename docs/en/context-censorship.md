# Context: Financial Censorship in the Digital World

> **Status:** Phase 0 — Historical and contextual reference document.  
> This document collects the documented cases that motivated the creation of Ludix.  
> This is not an opinion. These are facts, with sources.

---

## Why this document exists

Ludix was not born from an abstract theory about decentralization.  
It was born from observing, again and again, how private payment companies exercise censorship power over legal digital content — without trial, without tribunal, without transparency.

This document records the most relevant cases of that financial censorship.  
Some directly affect video game distribution.  
Others are part of the broader pattern that makes this problem structural, not anecdotal.

---

## Case 1: WikiLeaks (2010)

**The first globally scaled case of coordinated financial censorship.**

In November 2010, WikiLeaks began publishing diplomatic cables from the U.S. State Department.  
No criminal charges were filed against the organization in any country.  
No court ordered any action.

Yet in December 2010:

- **PayPal** closed WikiLeaks' account on December 4th.
- **Mastercard** suspended all payments to WikiLeaks on December 7th.
- **Visa** did the same that same day.
- **Bank of America** and **Western Union** followed days later.

The result was that WikiLeaks' funding dropped by **95%** virtually overnight.  
In 2011, Julian Assange announced that without resolving the blockade, the organization could not continue operating.

Most importantly: **no court ordered this blockade.**  
Mastercard justified the move by saying their rules "prohibit facilitating illegal activities" — but WikiLeaks had not been convicted of any crime in any country.

WikiLeaks litigated for years. In 2013, an Icelandic court ruled in their favor and ordered Valitor (a Visa and Mastercard partner in Iceland) to resume payment processing.  
It was a legal victory, but the damage was already done.

**The lesson:** A handful of private companies can financially strangle any organization, without going through any judicial process, simply by invoking their "internal policies."

**Documented sources:**
- WikiLeaks Banking Blockade (wikileaks.org)
- Reporters Without Borders — condemnation of the financial blockade
- Electronic Frontier Foundation — analysis of the precedent
- Supreme Court of Iceland ruling, 2013

## Case 2: Pornhub and the adult content industry (2020–2021)

**The case that demonstrated payment processors can act as moral arbiters of legal content.**

In December 2020, journalist Nicholas Kristof published a New York Times column denouncing the presence of illegal content (child sexual abuse material and non-consensual videos) on Pornhub.

The response was immediate:

- **Mastercard** suspended all payments to Pornhub on December 10, 2020.
- **Visa** did the same that same day.
- **Discover** followed.
- **American Express** had a pre-existing prohibition.
- **PayPal** had cut service in 2019.

Pornhub responded by removing millions of videos uploaded by unverified users and tightening moderation policies — measures that many independent sex workers had been requesting for years.

**The structural problem:**  
The blockade did not discriminate between illegal content and legal content. It affected **hundreds of thousands of legal adult content creators** who used the platform as their income source. Many lost their livelihoods overnight.

In April 2021, Mastercard went further: it established **new global requirements** for any site selling adult content, requiring age verification, consent verification, and specific moderation systems — defined by Mastercard, not by any law.

The Electronic Frontier Foundation described it directly:  
> This is the question of how much censorship power we want to give to payment processors.  
> Visa and Mastercard don't have the skills, expertise, or standing to make complex digital expression decisions.

**The lesson:** A payment processor can become a de facto regulator of entire industries, defining what content can exist on the internet — not based on laws, but on their own reputational risk policy.

**Documented sources:**
- New York Times — Nicholas Kristof column, December 2020
- Electronic Frontier Foundation — "Visa and Mastercard are Trying to Dictate What You Can Watch" (December 2020)
- CNN Business — "Mastercard, Visa and Discover cut ties with Pornhub" (December 2020)
- The Hill — "Mastercard updates policy for adult content sellers" (April 2021)

## Case 3: Steam and itch.io — Financial censorship reaches video games (2025)

**The case that directly motivated Ludix. It happened more recently than most people realize.**

In July 2025, an Australian organization called **Collective Shout** — self-described as an anti-pornography movement — launched a campaign specifically targeting the payment processors of Steam and itch.io.

The trigger was a game called *No Mercy* (already banned in April), but the campaign went much further: Collective Shout claimed to have found "hundreds of games with rape, incest, and abuse themes" on both platforms, and began directly pressuring Stripe, PayPal, Visa, and Mastercard.

**What happened on Steam:**  
Valve announced it would begin removing games that "violate rules and standards established by Steam's payment processors and their associated card networks and banks."  
Steam's original policy, which had been relatively clear, was replaced by a vague reference to third-party policies — which nobody can audit because they are private.

**What happened on itch.io:**  
The response was more chaotic — and more revealing.

On July 24, 2025, itch.io **deindexed without prior notice** all adult and NSFW content from its search and browsing platform. Not just explicit games: any content tagged as "adult", "NSFW", "erotica", or "sensitive media" disappeared from results.

itch.io founder Leaf Corcoran explained in the official statement:

> "Our ability to process payments is critical to every creator on our platform. To ensure we can continue operating and providing a marketplace for all developers, we must prioritize our relationship with our payment partners."

And later:

> "The situation developed rapidly, and we had to act urgently to protect the platform's core payment infrastructure. Unfortunately, this meant it was not realistic to give creators advance notice before making this change."

**The collateral damage was immediate:**  
Among the deindexed games was *Consume Me*, winner of the **Seumas McNally Grand Prize** at GDC — the most prestigious indie award in the industry. It was not an explicit game. It simply had a tag that the system interpreted as "sensitive."

Other works by LGBTQ+ artists, erotic novels, and TTRPGs with adult themes also disappeared without warning.

Meanwhile, itch.io confirmed that **Stripe had been suspended for 18+ content** and that they were seeking "other payment processors more willing to work with this type of content."

A **Change.org petition** asking Visa and Mastercard to stop "censoring legal fictional content that complies with the law and platform standards" gathered more than 50,000 verified signatures within days.

**The lesson:**  
It doesn't matter the size of the platform. It doesn't matter how long they've been operating. When a lobby organization with 1,000 people making calls pressures payment processors, platforms yield — because they cannot afford to lose their ability to collect payments.

**Documented sources:**
- Engadget — "itch.io is removing NSFW games to comply with payment processors' rules" (July 2025)
- PC Gamer — "itch.io is actively reaching out to other payment processors" (July 2025)
- The Gamer — "Payment Processors Censoring Itch.io And Steam Is Just The Start" (July 2025)
- Game Developer — "itch.io deindexing adult content to appease payments providers" (July 2025)
- Official itch.io statement (Leaf Corcoran, July 2025)

## The pattern connecting all cases

These three cases occurred in different contexts, with different organizations, and affected different industries.  
But they share exactly the same mechanism:

1. **An external entity** (government, lobby, organized campaign) pressures payment processors.
2. **Payment processors** — Visa, Mastercard, Stripe, PayPal — respond by suspending or threatening to suspend services.
3. **The platform**, which cannot operate without processing payments, **yields without judicial process** — sometimes within hours.
4. **Content disappears** — without a court order, without real appeal, without transparency.
5. **Creators** lose income, audience, and access to their own work.

What none of these cases had:
- A prior judicial ruling.
- A democratic process.
- A law that explicitly ordered it.
- Transparency about who made the decision and why.

## The distinction Ludix makes — and that platforms couldn't make

It's important to be honest about something uncomfortable:

**Not all the content removed in the documented cases was innocent.**

In the Pornhub case, there was genuinely illegal content — sexual abuse material,  
non-consensual videos — mixed in with the legal work of thousands of creators.  
The problem was not that the illegal content was removed.  
The problem was the mechanism: a payment processor acting as judge,  
without distinguishing between illegal and legal, removing everything at once,  
without process, without transparency, without any possibility of appeal.

The same happened on itch.io: alongside genuinely problematic content,  
award-winning games, artworks, novels, and projects by LGBTQ+ creators  
disappeared — creators who had done nothing wrong.

Ludix does not ignore that reality. It confronts it with a clear distinction:

---

### Three categories. Three different responses.

**Category 1 — Universally illegal**  
CSAM, terrorism, fraud, child sexual abuse material.  
Out of Ludix. No process. No discussion.  
There is global consensus and clear laws in virtually every country.

**Category 2 — Illegal in a specific jurisdiction**  
If a government issues a legitimate legal notification — with a democratic,  
transparent process and due process — Ludix complies.  
But *only for that jurisdiction*.  
A game illegal in Saudi Arabia does not disappear for a player in Germany.  
A game illegal in one U.S. state does not disappear for all of Latin America.  
The law has territorial reach. So does Ludix.

**Category 3 — Legal, but uncomfortable for private companies**  
This is where the real problem lies in every documented case.  
Legal content in most jurisdictions, removed not by a law or a judge,  
but because a private company decided it didn't fit their internal reputational risk policy.

For this category, Ludix has a simple answer: **No.**

---

### Ludix's golden rule on content

> If a government, through a legitimate, democratic, and transparent legal process,  
> notifies that certain content violates the laws of their jurisdiction —  
> that content is removed for that jurisdiction.  
>  
> If a private company, without democratic mandate or court order,  
> pressures to remove legal content — that pressure carries no authority here.

Ludix accepts laws.  
Ludix does not accept Visa replacing a judge.  
Ludix does not accept Mastercard replacing a parliament.  
Ludix does not accept a lobby campaign replacing a democratic process.

That distinction is non-negotiable. It is the core of why this project exists.

## The underlying problem: the concentration of payment power

In Europe, Visa and Mastercard jointly control more than **97% of the card payment market**.  
In most countries around the world, alternatives are minimal or nonexistent for digital transactions at scale.

This means that two private companies, headquartered in the United States, with their own internal risk policies, can:

- Cut off financial access to any digital platform.
- Do it without a court order.
- Do it within hours.
- Do it in a coordinated way, creating an effective blockade impossible to circumvent.

The EFF described it clearly in 2020:

> "Until we build a diverse and robust market of online payment services that doesn't depend on Visa and Mastercard, we have to deal with the fact that these two companies can dictate what you can read online."

Five years later, in the context of video games, that scenario materialized exactly as described.

---

## Why Ludix is the technical answer to this problem

Ludix cannot change the policies of Visa or Mastercard.  
Ludix cannot force Steam or itch.io to resist pressure.  
Ludix cannot legislate.

What Ludix can do is build an infrastructure where that pressure  
**has no lever to pull**:

- If payments go directly from player to developer in stablecoins, there is no payment processor to pressure.
- If builds are hosted on the developer's servers, there is no central platform that can deindex them.
- If the code is open source and forkable, there is no "off switch" that an organized campaign can find.

This does not solve every problem.  
But it eliminates the central point of failure that made all the documented cases in this file possible.
